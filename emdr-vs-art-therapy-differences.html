EMDR vs. ART Therapy: What Are the Differences?

November 13, 2026

Choosing trauma therapy can feel overwhelming, especially when treatment names sound similar. You may have heard about Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) and Accelerated Resolution Therapy (ART) and wondered whether they are essentially the same treatment.

EMDR and ART are distinct therapies that use eye movements while a person focuses on distressing experiences. EMDR follows an eight-phase treatment framework. ART uses a separate protocol that includes guided imagery and voluntary image replacement. EMDR also has a larger research base for treating post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). American Psychological Association.

Understanding the differences between EMDR and ART can help you ask informed questions. You do not need to decide on a treatment before contacting a therapist. A thoughtful assessment is the starting point for finding an approach that fits your needs.

What Is EMDR Therapy?

EMDR stands for Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing. It is a structured psychotherapy used to help people process distressing memories and reduce the difficulties associated with them.

During part of treatment, a person brings a selected memory to mind while following a therapist-guided back-and-forth movement or another form of bilateral stimulation. Depending on the protocol and clinical needs, this may involve eye movements, alternating sounds, or taps.

The goal is to change how distressing the memory feels and address associated beliefs and physical reactions. Treatment does not require forgetting what happened. VA National Center for PTSD.

What Are the Eight Phases of EMDR?

The American Psychological Association describes eight phases of EMDR: history-taking, preparation, assessment, desensitization, installation, body scan, closure, and reevaluation.

In everyday terms, the therapist learns about your concerns, helps you prepare, identifies treatment targets, guides memory processing, and checks your response. Preparation and follow-up are part of the treatment, alongside the eye movements people often associate with EMDR. American Psychological Association.

What Is Accelerated Resolution Therapy?

Accelerated Resolution Therapy is a separate psychotherapy approach that combines therapist-guided eye movements with work on distressing sensations and mental images. In this context, ART does not mean art therapy involving drawing or painting.

A distinctive ART technique is voluntary image replacement. With guidance, the client imagines a preferred version of a distressing scene or a different image of their choosing. The person understands that the imagined changes do not change the historical facts.

For example, an imagined scene might include a sense of protection or a different ending. This is a general illustration of imagery work, rather than a prediction of what a particular session will involve.

The ART developer’s description of voluntary image replacement explains this feature. Descriptions from a treatment’s developer can clarify its procedures; independent evidence is needed to evaluate claims about effectiveness. ART: Voluntary Image Replacement.

The therapies share some tools, but training in one does not automatically establish competence in the other. Ask specifically about your therapist’s training and experience with the approach being recommended.

Is EMDR or ART Better Supported by Research?

For PTSD, EMDR has stronger established research support. The 2023 VA/DoD PTSD clinical practice guideline recommends EMDR among its strongly recommended trauma-focused psychotherapies. That guideline found insufficient evidence to recommend for or against ART. 2023 VA/DoD PTSD Clinical Practice Guideline.

That distinction does not mean ART cannot help. It means the guideline’s evidence review did not support an equally strong recommendation. A treatment may be promising while researchers are still examining its benefits, limitations, and longer-term outcomes.

It would therefore be misleading to describe ART as proven superior to EMDR, or to say that EMDR is the right choice for every person. Ask your clinician to explain the evidence relevant to your situation and discuss other appropriate options as well.

Does ART Work Faster Than EMDR?

ART is designed as a brief treatment, but its name does not establish that it will work faster for you. Comparing session counts from different studies is also not the same as directly comparing two treatments in similar patients.

Your treatment plan should account for your goals, history, current circumstances, and response to care. Ask whether an estimated session count includes assessment and preparation, or refers only to work on one specific memory.

Instead of focusing only on finishing quickly, discuss what meaningful progress would look like. Perhaps your priority is feeling more comfortable during daily activities, responding differently to reminders, or improving your ability to participate in family life.

Do You Have to Describe Every Detail of Your Trauma?

Neither approach should be presented as requiring a detailed spoken account of every event. The VA’s patient guide to EMDR explains that people generally do not need to describe their trauma in detail, although they are asked to think about it during treatment. ART also allows imagery processing without narrating every detail aloud. VA National Center for PTSD.

This does not mean the therapist needs no background information. Assessment, treatment planning, and monitoring your well-being remain important. If privacy or talking about the event worries you, say so at the beginning. Ask what information is necessary and how the therapist will help you communicate discomfort.

What Should Parents Ask About Trauma Therapy for Children or Teens?

If you are researching EMDR vs. ART therapy for your child, ask how the clinician adapts treatment to a young person’s developmental level. Adult research and treatment timelines should not automatically be applied to children.

Bring questions such as:

  • What experience do you have treating children or teens of this age?
  • What training do you have in the approach you recommend?
  • How will you determine whether my child is ready?
  • What role will parents or caregivers have?
  • How will you explain the process and check my child’s comfort?
  • How will we review progress and decide whether to adjust the plan?

You can begin by describing what you have noticed and what concerns your child has expressed. You do not need to arrive with a diagnosis or a chosen therapy.

How Do You Choose Between EMDR and ART?

Start with a qualified clinician who can explain the recommendation, discuss alternatives, and invite questions. Ask how they will assess your symptoms, consider your preferences, and monitor whether treatment is helping.

It is reasonable to ask what a session looks like, what you might experience afterward, and how to contact the practice with concerns. Trauma-focused work can bring up uncomfortable feelings. Discuss preparation, pacing, and what happens if you need to pause.

Cost, insurance coverage, travel, and scheduling also matter. Confirm those details before treatment begins, including whether the clinician and service are available at your preferred office.
